\begin{abstract}
Standard metaheuristics like Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) act like water flowing downhill: they blindly follow local gradients (pheromones) and often settle into suboptimal valleys (stagnation).
We propose \emph{Active Trajectory Shaping}, a framework that transforms the neural model from a static initializer into an active \emph{navigator}.
Instead of relying solely on past success (pheromone) to guide the future, our method injects "future intuition" by observing the global \emph{search context}, the evolving pheromone landscape and incumbent solution, and actively bending the search trajectory away from stagnation and toward promising regions.
Technically, we implement this via a \emph{Dynamic Trajectory Guidance} mechanism that modulates edge transition probabilities in real-time.
Coupled with a scalable Perturbative ACO design and Smoothed MMAS normalization, our approach demonstrates that a neural navigator can effectively shape the optimization process, achieving competitive results on TSP and CVRP by learning to intervene exactly when the search falters.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) methods are commonly categorized into \emph{constructive} and \emph{hybrid / iterative} approaches
and have recently been successfully applied to routing problems such as the \TSP{} and \VRP{}.
Constructive methods aim to learn an end-to-end solver that directly outputs a solution, whereas hybrid or improvement-based methods build on top of
existing solvers or heuristics.

\ACO{} remains one of the most effective metaheuristics for \TSP{} and \VRP{}.
However, standard \ACO{} suffers from a fundamental limitation: it is a "blind" process. Ants follow pheromone trails that represent \emph{past} successes, effectively hill-climbing on a heuristic landscape. When this landscape leads to a local optimum, the colony stagnates, lacking the global perspective to escape.
Traditional fixes involve "resets" or "mutation", which are random and undirected.

\emph{The Neural Navigator.}
We argue that overcoming stagnation requires a shift from passive heuristics to active \emph{trajectory shaping}.
DeepACO~\cite{deepaco} and successors introduced neural priors, but mostly as static heatmaps (Open-loop) that do not adapt to the search progress.
In contrast, our approach functions as a navigator. It monitors the \emph{Search Context}, the distribution of pheromones and the structure of the current best solution, and dynamically injects bias to warp the search space.
For example, if the colony converges too prematurely (low pheromone entropy), the navigator can "heat up" exploration in specific regions. If the search is too diffuse, it can focus the beam.

\paragraph{Main idea.}
We propose a \emph{dynamic neural prior over the metaheuristic state} that modulates \ACO{} transition probabilities in a closed loop.

% ,  NEW: crisp definition box for reviewers , 
\DefBox{
\emph{Definition (Open-loop vs Closed-loop neural ACO).}
Let $x$ denote a problem instance and let $s_t$ denote the solver state at \ACO{} iteration $t$ (including pheromone and incumbent/best-solution information).
\emph{Open-loop} neural \ACO{} computes guidance $g_\theta(\cdot; x)$ that is fixed for the duration of a run (or depends only on $x$).
\emph{Closed-loop} neural \ACO{} computes guidance $g_\theta(\cdot; s_t)$ at multiple iterations $t$ and conditions on solver state, enabling feedback control
and adaptation to stagnation and trajectory changes.
}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Dynamic control of the search trajectory.}
    We condition a neural controller on both static instance features and dynamic solver features (pheromone and incumbent solution structure) and integrate its output
    as an additive log-space prior over \ACO{} transitions (\Cref{sec:method}).
    \item \emph{Smoothed \MMAS{} for stable learning.}
    We replace tour-length-based pheromone bounds with a deterministic, scale-robust bounding scheme in $[1/K, 1]$ and use membership-based updates
    to reduce instance sensitivity (\Cref{sec:smoothed-mmas}).
    \item \emph{Local search in the training loop with localized credit assignment.}
    We incorporate LS into training and reduce variance via \emph{localized} LS restricted to modified regions, enabling more meaningful credit assignment
    under policy gradients (\Cref{sec:ls-training}).
    \item \emph{Extension beyond \TSP{}.}
    We extend the localized-LS framework to \CVRP{} and demonstrate effectiveness across both domains (\Cref{sec:cvrp}).
    \item \emph{Fair evaluation under matched budgets.}
    We report results under matched wall-clock budgets and matched LS budgets, and quantify controller overhead (\Cref{sec:fairness}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Preliminaries}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Problems: \TSP{} and \CVRP{}}
We consider the Euclidean TSP defined on a graph $G=(V, E)$ with $V=\{1, \dots, n\}$ nodes. The goal is to find a Hamiltonian cycle $\pi$ minimizing $C(\pi) = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} d(\pi_i, \pi_{i+1}) + d(\pi_n, \pi_1)$, where $d(\cdot, \cdot)$ is the Euclidean distance.
For CVRP, we solve for a set of routes covering all customers starting and ending at a depot (node 0), subject to capacity constraints. We generate random instances where coordinates are sampled uniformly in $[0, 1]^2$.

\subsection{Ant Colony Optimization}
\label{sec:aco}
Ant Colony Optimization (\ACO{}) constructs solutions probabilistically.
At each step, an ant at node $i$ chooses the next node $j$ from its feasible neighborhood $\mathcal{N}(i)$ using the transition rule:
\begin{equation}
p(j \mid i) = \frac{[\tau_{ij}]^\alpha [\eta_{ij}]^\beta}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}(i)} [\tau_{ik}]^\alpha [\eta_{ik}]^\beta}, \quad j \in \mathcal{N}(i).
\label{eq:aco-transition}
\end{equation}
Here, $\tau_{ij}$ is the pheromone trail intensity, $\eta_{ij} = 1/d_{ij}$ is the heuristic visibility, and $\alpha, \beta$ control their relative influence.
We operate on a sparse candidate graph (K-nearest neighbors) to scale to large instances.

\subsection{\MMAS{} and Stagnation}
\emph{Max-Min Ant System (\MMAS{})} \cite{stutzle2000max} improves standard \ACO{} by bounding pheromones $\tau_{ij} \in [\tau_{\min}, \tau_{\max}]$ to prevent premature convergence.
Only the best ant (global-best or iteration-best) is allowed to deposit pheromone.
Despite these mechanisms, stagnation, where $\tau$ converges to a suboptimal path and exploration ceases, remains a key challenge, often requiring problem-specific restart strategies.

\subsection{Local Search for Routing}
To achieve competitive performance, modern \ACO{} pipelines integrate Local Search (LS).
For TSP, 2-opt and 3-opt are standard. For CVRP, RELOCATE and SWAP operators are commonly used.
While LS significantly improves quality, it complicates learning because it decouples the neural policy's discrete choices from the final reward.

\section{Active Trajectory Shaping}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{ACO as a Controlled Markov Process with Sample-and-Hold Control}
\label{sec:process}
We formalize closed-loop neural \ACO{} as a controlled stochastic process with a \emph{control period} $S$ (sample-and-hold).
Let $\ell = 0,1,\dots,L-1$ index \emph{control updates}, where $L=\lfloor T/S \rfloor$.

\paragraph{Macro-state.}
Define the macro-state at control update $\ell$ as
\[
S_\ell := s_{\ell S} = (x, \tau^{(\ell S)}, b^{(\ell S)}, \texttt{cfg}, \xi_{\ell S}),
\]
where $x$ is the instance, $\tau$ pheromones on the candidate graph, $b$ the incumbent, \texttt{cfg} fixed hyperparameters, and $\xi$ persistent solver memory.

\paragraph{Macro-action (held for $S$ inner steps).}
At each control update, the navigator outputs a logit-shaping field
\[
A_\ell := z_\theta(S_\ell) \in \R^{n\times K}.
\]
This action is held constant for the next $S$ inner ACO iterations (sample-and-hold).

\paragraph{Inner evolution.}
Given $A_\ell$, the solver executes $S$ inner ACO iterations (sampling ants under the shaped kernel, applying LS, updating incumbent, updating pheromones), yielding the next macro-state:
\[
S_{\ell+1} = s_{(\ell+1)S} \sim \mathcal{P}_S(\cdot \mid S_\ell, A_\ell),
\]
where $\mathcal{P}_S$ denotes the $S$-step transition induced by the inner procedure.

\begin{proposition}[Markov Property at Control Updates]
\label{prop:markov-macro}
The sample-and-hold closed-loop solver induces a controlled Markov chain on macro-states:
\[
\Pr(S_{\ell+1}\mid S_{0:\ell}, A_{0:\ell}) = \Pr(S_{\ell+1}\mid S_\ell, A_\ell)=\mathcal{P}_S(S_{\ell+1}\mid S_\ell, A_\ell).
\]
\end{proposition}


\subsection{Search Context Representation}
\label{sec:state}
The core of our approach is conditioning the neural navigator on the global \emph{Search Context} $s_t$, rather than just the problem instance.
We define the context over the sparse candidate graph using:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Static features:} Node embeddings $h_i$ derived from a pre-trained encoder (e.g., GNN) on the distance graph.
    \item \emph{Dynamic Context features:} For each candidate edge $(i, j)$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Pheromone strength: $\log(\tau_{ij})$, normalized by row statistics (z-score), representing the \emph{past search history}.
        \item Pheromone rank: Rank of $\tau_{ij}$ among neighbors of $i$, scaled to $[0, 1]$.
        \item Incumbent indicator: $\mathbb{I}\{(i, j) \in b_{t-1}\}$, representing the \emph{current best path}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
These features allow the navigator to detect stagnation (e.g., low pheromone entropy, unchanging incumbent) and intervene by shaping the trajectory.

\subsection{Dynamic Trajectory Guidance}
\label{sec:controller}
The neural navigator $\pi_{\theta}$ outputs a scalar guidance score $g_{\theta}(i, j; s_t)$ for each candidate edge.
To ensure robustness to scale, we normalize these scores per node to obtain a zero-mean, unit-variance residual $z_{ij} = (g_{ij} - \mu_i)/(\sigma_i + \epsilon)$.
The modified \ACO{} transition kernel operates in log-space:
\begin{equation}
\log \tilde{p}_t(j \mid i) = \alpha \log \tau^{(t)}_{ij} + \beta \log \eta_{ij} + z_{ij} - \log Z_i,
\label{eq:logspace-prior}
\end{equation}
where $Z_i$ is the partition function. This formulation can be rigorously interpreted as \emph{exponential tilting} of the baseline kernel.

\paragraph{Exponential Tilting View.}
Let $q_t(\cdot\mid i)$ be the baseline ACO transition distribution without the neural term.
\begin{lemma}[Exponential Tilting]
The shaped transition distribution satisfies
\[
p^\theta_t(j\mid i)=\frac{q_t(j\mid i)\exp(z_{ij})}{\mathbb{E}_{k\sim q_t(\cdot\mid i)}[\exp(z_{ik})]}.
\]
\end{lemma}
This admits a \emph{variational characterization} that makes the controller's role mathematically concrete:
\begin{equation}
p^\theta_t(\cdot\mid i) = \arg\max_{p \in \Delta(\mathcal{N}(i))}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{j\sim p}[z_{ij}] - \mathrm{KL}(p(\cdot\mid i)\,\|\,q_t(\cdot\mid i))\Big),
\label{eq:kl-regularized}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta(\mathcal{N}(i))$ is the probability simplex over the candidate neighborhood of node $i$.
Thus, the navigator performs a \emph{KL-regularized policy improvement step} around the baseline ACO kernel: it finds the distribution closest to $q_t$ (in KL divergence) that maximizes the expected guidance score $z$. This is not an arbitrary probability hack---it is the optimal soft update given the controller's ``advice,'' and the domain constraint ties directly to support preservation on the candidate graph.

\paragraph{Nodewise Normalization.}
The per-node normalization of $g$ to $z$ (using rowwise mean and standard deviation) introduces a within-row competition term: guidance scores are coupled across neighbors of each node. When $\sigma_i \approx 0$ (uniform guidance), the $\epsilon$ term in the denominator clamps the controller influence, providing an intended stability feature that prevents amplification of noise when the network is uncertain.

\begin{definition}[Trajectory Shaping (Operational Goal)]
Let $\{s_t\}_{t=0}^T$ be the stochastic process induced by standard ACO (baseline) and $\{\tilde{s}_t\}_{t=0}^T$ be the process induced by our method. Define a stagnation indicator $\mathsf{stag}(s_t) \in \{0,1\}$ (e.g., pheromone entropy below threshold, or no incumbent improvement for $L$ consecutive steps). We say the navigator performs \emph{Trajectory Shaping} if:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)] \emph{Stagnation-conditional control:} The navigator exerts control effort specifically when search falters:
    \[
    \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathsf{stag}(s_t)\cdot \mathrm{KL}(p^\theta_t(\cdot\mid i)\,\|\,q_t(\cdot\mid i))\right] > 0 \quad \text{for some nodes } i;
    \]
    \item[(ii)] \emph{Performance improvement:} The expected final cost improves: $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}(\tilde{b}_T)] < \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}(b_T)]$.
\end{enumerate}
Condition (i) aligns with our headline claim: the navigator ``intervenes exactly when the search falters.''
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Support Preservation on Candidate Graph]
If $\tau_{ij} \in [\tau_{\min}, \tau_{\max}]$ with $\tau_{\min} > 0$, $\eta_{ij} > 0$, and $z_{ij}$ is finite, then $\tilde{p}(j \mid i) > 0$ for all candidates $j \in \mathcal{N}(i)$.
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Scope of Support.} We emphasize that support is preserved \emph{relative to the candidate graph and feasibility mask}. The sparse $K$-nearest-neighbor construction already restricts the reachable tour space (tours requiring non-candidate edges have zero probability under both baseline and shaped kernels). Under perturbative sampling (Section~\ref{sec:perturbative}), exploration is further restricted to an incumbent-centered neighborhood per iteration; the proposition guarantees no additional zeros \emph{within that neighborhood's candidate choices}. This does not imply global ergodicity---rather, it ensures the navigator cannot collapse local exploration to determinism.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
Our neural navigator $\pi_{\theta}$ consists of two main components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Encoder (GNN):} A 12-layer Graph Neural Network that processes static instance features (coordinates). It includes residual connections and Batch Normalization to ensure deep signal propagation. The encoder produces node embeddings $h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$.
    \item \emph{Decoder (MLP):} A 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) that maps edge features (concatenated source/target node embeddings and dynamic search context features) to the scalar guidance logit $g_{\theta}(i, j)$.
\end{itemize}
This architecture balances representational power with the speed required for iterative sampling.

\subsection{Activation Schedule}
\label{sec:activation}
To balance computational overhead and control frequency, we employ an activation schedule. In our experiments, we activate the controller every iteration, as the lightweight MLP decoder is negligible compared to the cost of sampling and local search.

\subsection{Smoothed \MMAS{} Pheromone Dynamics}
\label{sec:smoothed-mmas}
Standard \MMAS{} dynamically adjusts bounds $[\tau_{\min}, \tau_{\max}]$ based on the best tour cost, making the dynamics sensitive to objective scaling.
We propose a \emph{Smoothed \MMAS{}} update that stabilizes learning by using fixed relative targets.
Let $B_t$ be the set of edges in the best solution used for updates (iteration-best or global-best).
We update each pheromone value $\tau_{ij}$ towards a target value:
\begin{equation}
\tau^{(t+1)}_{ij} \leftarrow (1-\rho)\tau^{(t)}_{ij} + \rho \cdot \mathcal{T}_{ij},
\label{eq:smoothed-mmas}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_{ij} = \tau_{\max}$ if $(i,j) \in B_t$ and $\mathcal{T}_{ij} = \tau_{\min}$ otherwise.
Typically, we set $\tau_{\max}=1$ and $\tau_{\min}=1/K$, independent of the instance cost scale.
This linear interpolation rule ensures pheromones remain within bounds and evolve smoothly, providing a stable signal for the neural controller.

\paragraph{Smoothed-MMAS Stability.}
\emph{Proposition 2 (Bounds Invariance).} If $\tau^{(0)}_{ij}\in[\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max}]$ for all $(i,j)$, then $\tau^{(t)}_{ij}\in[\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max}]$ for all $t \ge 0$.

\emph{Proposition 3 (Geometric Convergence).} If the set of update edges $B_{t}=B$ remains constant for $h$ consecutive updates, then
\[
\tau_{ij}^{(t+h)}=(1-\rho)^h \tau_{ij}^{(t)} + \left(1-(1-\rho)^h\right)\mathcal{T}_{ij}.
\]
In particular, $\tau^{(t+h)}_{ij}\to \mathcal{T}_{ij}$ geometrically as $h\to\infty$. This guarantees that the solver state converges efficiently when a stable optimum is found.

\paragraph{Trade-off Discussion.}
Smoothed-MMAS turns pheromone dynamics into a \emph{stable linear system} with a single fixed point (for fixed $B_t$). When $B_t$ varies over time, Smoothed-MMAS forms a bounded \emph{switched affine system}; each step remains a contraction toward the current target matrix $\mathcal{T}(B_t)$:
\[
\|\tau^{(t+1)}-\mathcal{T}(B_t)\|_\infty \le (1-\rho)\|\tau^{(t)}-\mathcal{T}(B_t)\|_\infty,
\]
preserving stability and boundedness regardless of how the best solution changes.
Because $\tau_{\min}=1/K$ and $\tau_{\max}=1$, the controller observes \emph{scale-stable inputs}, so learning is not confounded by instance objective scale. We note that classic MMAS bounds are linked to tour cost and can be interpreted as adapting exploitation pressure to solution quality. Our scheme trades cost-calibrated pheromone magnitudes for stable closed-loop learning signals---a deliberate choice that empirically improves training stability without sacrificing final solution quality.

\subsection{Learning Objective and Optimization}
\label{sec:training}
We define our training objective as minimizing the expected cost accumulated over the search trajectory. Let $\hat{\pi}_{a,t} = \mathrm{LS}(\pi_{a,t})$ denote the post-local-search tour. While the true goal is minimizing the final incumbent cost $\mathbb{E}[C(b_T)]$, we optimize a dense surrogate: the expected per-iteration post-LS cost, which reduces variance and provides frequent feedback:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s_{0:T}, a_{0:T}} \left[ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{1}{m} \sum_{a=1}^m C(\hat{\pi}_{a,t}) \right].
\end{equation}
We overload notation and denote by $\pi_\theta(\pi \mid s_t)$ the induced probability of sampling tour $\pi$ under the shaped transition kernel defined by $z_\theta(s_t)$.
We estimate the gradient $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ using the score function estimator:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{1}{m} \sum_{a=1}^m (C(\hat{\pi}_{a,t}) - b_t) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\pi_{a,t} \mid s_t),
\end{equation}
where $b_t$ is a baseline and we differentiate through $\pi_{a,t}$ (the pre-LS sampling), not through $\mathrm{LS}$. Crucially, this estimator remains valid even with non-differentiable components like Local Search:

\begin{proposition}[Gradient Unbiasedness with Local Search]
Let the reward be $R_t = \text{Cost}(\text{LS}(\pi_t))$, where $\text{LS}$ is an arbitrary non-differentiable local search operator. The score-function estimator $\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}[R_t] = \mathbb{E}[(R_t - b_t) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\pi_t | s_t)]$ remains unbiased for any baseline $b_t$ independent of the sampled trace $\pi_t$ (conditional on $s_t$), as the LS operator is part of the environment transition and does not affect the validity of the log-derivative trick.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Baseline Choice and Bias Considerations.}
In practice (Algorithm~\ref{alg:train}), we use a \emph{leave-one-out} baseline to ensure unbiasedness while retaining variance reduction. For each ant $a$:
\begin{equation}
b_{k}^{(-a)} = \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{a'\ne a} C(\hat{\pi}_{a',k}), \quad A_{a,k} = C(\hat{\pi}_{a,k}) - b_k^{(-a)}.
\end{equation}
This removes the $O(1/m)$ bias that arises when each sample's baseline includes its own cost, while preserving the variance reduction benefits of batch normalization. We note that the surrogate objective (average cost over iterations) is a reward-shaping choice that correlates with final incumbent quality while providing denser gradient signal; empirically, it improves $\mathcal{C}(b_T)$.
We accumulate gradients over a short horizon (e.g., $T=10$) before updating weights.
This "Online REINFORCE" approach directly optimizes the controller to generate search trajectories that minimize cost at every step.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Online REINFORCE Training}
\label{alg:train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Initialize policy $\theta$, optimizer
\FOR{epoch $e = 1, \dots, E$}
    \FOR{step $= 1, \dots, \text{Steps}$}
        \STATE Sample random instance $x$
        \STATE Initialize \ACO{}
        \FOR{outer\_iter $t = 1$ to $T/S$}
            \STATE Build features $s_t$; Predict prior $g_\theta(s_t)$
            \STATE Loss accumulator $\mathcal{L}_{t} \leftarrow 0$
            \FOR{inner\_iter $k = 1$ to $S$}
                \STATE Sample $m$ ants $\pi_{a,k}$ (recording log-probs $\log p_{a,k}$)
                \STATE Apply LS: $\hat{\pi}_{a,k} \leftarrow \mathrm{LS}(\pi_{a,k})$; compute costs $C(\hat{\pi}_{a,k})$
                \STATE Leave-one-out baseline: $b_k^{(-a)} = \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{a'\ne a} C(\hat{\pi}_{a',k})$
                \STATE Advantage $A_{a,k} = C(\hat{\pi}_{a,k}) - b_k^{(-a)}$
                \STATE $\mathcal{L}_{t} \leftarrow \mathcal{L}_{t} + \frac{1}{m}\sum_a A_{a,k} \log p_{a,k}$
                \STATE Update pheromone and incumbent
            \ENDFOR
            \STATE \emph{Backpropagate} $\mathcal{L}_{t}$ (accumulating gradients)
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE \emph{Optimizer.Step()} (Gradient Clipping 1.0)
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Scalable Perturbative Search and Localized Training}
\label{sec:perturbative}

\subsection{The Challenge of Global Sampling}
Standard ACO constructs complete solutions from scratch at every iteration. While effective for small graphs, this $O(N)$ constructive process becomes a bottleneck for large-scale routing ($N > 1000$), especially when integrated with expensive Local Search (LS) routines. Furthermore, global LS operators (e.g., full 2-opt) can break the credit assignment in RL by overwriting the policy's specific edge choices.

\subsection{Perturbative Ant Colony}
To address both scalability and credit assignment, we adopt a \emph{Perturbative ACO} strategy. Instead of empty tours, ants are initialized with the incumbent solution. The ant then performs a ``random walk with repair'' to effectively \emph{perturb} the incumbent.
Crucially, we restrict the sampling budget using a \texttt{min\_new\_edges} constraint ($M$):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Stop sampling when } \text{count}(\text{new\_edges}) \ge M.
\end{equation}
By setting $M \ll N$ (e.g., $M=16$ for $N=100$), the \emph{number of modified edges and subsequent LS neighborhood size} scales with $M$ rather than $N$. This can be viewed as a \emph{learned large-neighborhood search (LNS) operator}, where the neighborhood is defined implicitly by the perturbation budget $M$ and candidate list $K$.

\paragraph{Complexity per Outer Iteration.}
We distinguish between active computation and auxiliary costs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Active ($O(MK)$):} Sampling $M$ edge modifications, neural MLP forward pass over $M$ edges, localized LS neighborhood scans ($M$ checklist nodes $\times$ $K$ candidates).
    \item \emph{Auxiliary ($O(N)$):} Maintaining full tour representation, visited-set bookkeeping, and (if not incremental) full cost evaluation.
\end{itemize}
The key insight is that the \emph{computationally intensive} operations (neural inference, sampling, LS) scale with $M \cdot K$, enabling CL-ACO to handle instances with 10k nodes even though auxiliary bookkeeping remains $O(N)$.

\paragraph{Restricted Reachability.}
Under perturbative sampling, the reachable tour space in a single iteration is restricted to a neighborhood around the incumbent. This is a deliberate design choice: we trade global reachability for tighter credit assignment. The trajectory shaping operates within this controlled neighborhood, steering local improvements rather than global reconstruction.

\subsection{Localized Local Search for Credit Assignment}
We couple this perturbative sampling with \emph{Localized LS}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Tracking:} We maintain a \texttt{checklist} of nodes incident to the $M$ new edges.
    \item \emph{Focused Optimization:} We apply restricted 2-opt only to edges involving nodes in the \texttt{checklist}.
\end{enumerate}
This design serves two purposes. First, it bounds the LS neighborhood: examining $O(M)$ checklist nodes against $O(K)$ candidate neighbors yields $O(M \cdot K)$ move evaluations per ant, compared to $O(N \cdot K)$ for global LS. Second, and more importantly for learning, the final reward (cost improvement) is tightly correlated with the specific edge choices made by the neural policy, rather than being dominated by global LS corrections. This localized credit assignment substantially reduces gradient variance and stabilizes REINFORCE training.

\section{Extension to \CVRP{}}
\label{sec:cvrp}
To demonstrate the generality of CL-ACO, we extend it to the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (\CVRP{}).
\subsection{Problem Representation}
We adopt the \emph{Giant Tour} representation, commonly used in metaheuristics for VRP.
A solution is represented as a permutation of all $n$ customer nodes. To evaluate the cost of a permutation, we employ the optimal \texttt{Split} algorithm \cite{prins2004}, which partitions the sequence into feasible routes (respecting capacity limits) and inserts the depot (node 0) optimally using a dynamic programming approach.
This reduces the CVRP search space to the space of permutations, allowing us to reuse the exact same ACO construction and Local Search operators as in TSP.
For efficiency, we implement a custom C++ version of Split that maintains prefix sums to evaluate 2-opt moves in $O(1)$. \emph{Full details of the Split algorithm and our implementation are provided in Appendix B.}

\subsection{Modifications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Graph:} The graph contains $n$ customer nodes. The depot is implicit in the objective evaluations.
    \item \emph{Feasibility Masking:} Identical to TSP (Hamiltonian path on customers).
    \item \emph{Localized LS:} The 2-opt operator optimizes the Euclidean length of the giant tour as a proxy, avoiding expensive split re-evaluations during the LS phase. The true CVRP cost is computed only once per ant after the LS phase.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Proxy Objective Mismatch.}
We acknowledge that improving Euclidean giant-tour length is not guaranteed to improve post-split CVRP cost; in principle, it can even hurt. This introduces a mismatch between the LS proxy and the true objective. Crucially, however, the proxy is used \emph{only as a proposal mechanism} within LS---the \textbf{reward signal for REINFORCE is always the true CVRP cost} computed via Split after each ant completes. This ensures that the policy gradient points toward genuine CVRP improvements, even if individual LS moves are guided by the proxy. Empirically, giant-tour geometry correlates well with CVRP quality for Euclidean instances, and our experiments (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) confirm that this approach yields competitive results despite the approximation.

We evaluate CL-ACO on random Euclidean TSP and CVRP instances of size $N \in \{100, 500, 1000, 5000, 10000\}$.

\subsection{Metrics}
In addition to optimality gap, we track metrics to quantify the neural controller's behavior:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Gap (\%):} Relative percentage gap to solution found by LKH-3 (TSP) or HGS (CVRP).
    \item \emph{Prior L2 Drift:} Relative $L_2$ change in the prior matrix between steps, measuring how aggressively the controller shifts guidance.
    \item \emph{KL Divergence:} Kullback-Leibler divergence between consecutive prior distributions, quantifying information gain.
    \item \emph{Turnover:} The fraction of the top-5\% probability edges that change between steps, indicating search focus shifts.
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Classical Solvers:}

\subsection{Analysis: Active Trajectory Shaping}
\label{sec:trajectory}
\paragraph{C1. Stagnation Intervention.} We analyze pheromone entropy during stagnation. CL-ACO prevents collapse by maintaining higher entropy when improving moves are scarce ("heating up").
\paragraph{C2. Counterfactual Control.} Freezing pheromones reveals that the neural navigator alone can drive search progress, while freezing the navigator degrades performance to open-loop baselines.
\paragraph{C3. Edge-Level Audits.} Probability mass analysis confirms the navigator effectively steers sampling towards the incumbent neighborhood without enforcing determinism.

\subsection{Runtime and Scalability}
The neural navigator adds approximately 15-20\% overhead per iteration. However, the faster convergence rate compensates for this, yielding better solutions within the same total time.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
CL-ACO demonstrates that a learned \emph{navigator} can successfully guide a "blind" metaheuristic by observing the global search context.
Limitations include the overhead of the GNN encoder and the need for careful hyperparameter tuning of the RL reward.
Future work includes applying Active Trajectory Shaping to other domains (Job Shop Scheduling) and exploring Transformers for richer context encoding.

\section{Conclusion}
We introduced a framework for \emph{Active Trajectory Shaping} in metaheuristics. By transforming the neural model into a dynamic navigator that conditions on Search Context, and training it via Snapshot RL with localized perturbations, we overcome the stagnation limits of standard ACO. Our results on TSP and CVRP show that "future intuition" can effectively guide "blind" search, paving the way for more autonomous and robust optimization solvers.

\section{CVRP Split Algorithm Details}
\label{app:split}
\emph{Giant Tour Representation:} 
We map the CVRP to a permutation problem by treating the solution as a single sequence of customers. The optimal cost for a given permutation is found by inserting the depot into the sequence to form valid routes.

\emph{Split Algorithm (Prins 2004):}
Let $P = (c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n)$ be the permutation of customers. We construct a directed acyclic graph $H$ where an edge $(i, j)$ exists if the subsequence $(c_{i+1}, \dots, c_j)$ corresponds to a valid route (load $\le$ Capacity). The weight of edge $(i, j)$ is the cost of that route plus the depot connections. Finding the shortest path from node 0 to node $n$ in $H$ yields the optimal route partition.
Let $V_i$ be the cost of the optimal partition for the first $i$ customers. The Bellman equation is:
\[ V_i = \min_{0 \le j < i} \{ V_j + \text{Cost}(c_{j+1}, \dots, c_i) \} \]
where $\text{Cost}(\cdot)$ includes the depot distances.

\emph{Efficient Local Search:}
To maximize efficiency, our Local Search (2-opt) optimizes the \emph{Euclidean length} of the giant tour as a proxy for the VRP objective. This allows us to evaluate neighbor moves in $O(1)$ using standard distance deltas. The true penalised VRP cost is computed only once per ant after the LS phase using an efficient \emph{Linear Split} algorithm ($O(N)$) implemented with a monotonic deque \cite{prins2004}, which avoids the quadratic complexity of the naïve Split.
