\begin{abstract}
Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) remains one of the most effective metaheuristics for routing problems such as TSP/VRP, benefiting from decades of algorithmic refinements including candidate lists, pheromone bounds, and embedded local search. A major practical weakness of ACO, however, is \emph{stagnation}: the colony can become trapped in local optima as pheromone mass concentrates, requiring expert-designed resets or diversification heuristics that are instance-dependent and difficult to tune robustly. Prior neural enhancements to ACO primarily learn \emph{open-loop} static priors, such as a heuristic matrix (a ``heatmap'') or an initialization of pheromones, without conditioning on the evolving pheromone dynamics and best-so-far feedback during the run.
We propose \emph{Closed-Loop ACO} (CL-ACO), a neural controller trained to steer the \emph{search trajectory} of ACO online. Instead of producing a static prior, our policy is activated throughout the run and conditions on the current pheromone state and search progress to reshape transition probabilities via a residual factor. We train this controller using snapshot-based reinforcement learning: from a saved ACO state, we compare the best cost after a fixed continuation horizon against a paired baseline continuation from the same snapshot (Baseline-vs-Baseline reward), enabling low-variance learning with cached baselines, traced sampling, and per-ant credit assignment. To keep data stable while still adapting to the current policy, we use a DAgger-style pipeline of \emph{frozen snapshot generations} mixed via replay. Experiments on TSP demonstrate that closed-loop control improves continuation quality, reduces sensitivity to stagnation, and achieves consistent gains without hand-crafted restart rules.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================

Neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) methods are commonly grouped into (i) \emph{constructive} approaches that learn an end-to-end solver mapping an instance to a complete solution, and (ii) \emph{hybrid / improvement} approaches that learn to guide, augment, or improve an existing solver trajectory. Constructive methods can be elegant but often face challenges in scaling, incorporating decades of heuristic knowledge, and matching the engineering performance of mature optimization pipelines. Hybrid methods instead treat the solver as a strong backbone and learn targeted improvements (e.g., predicting k-opt moves or local-improvement operators), frequently delivering better robustness and scalability.

In this paper we focus on Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), a long-standing and highly competitive metaheuristic for Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) and Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP). ACO has benefited from many improvements over the years, including candidate lists, bounding mechanisms (e.g., MMAS-style trail limits), and the integration of local search. These refinements allow ACO variants to solve large-scale instances effectively; for example, Focused ACO-style ideas emphasize selectively allocating computation to promising regions of the search.

Despite these strengths, ACO suffers from a fundamental practical issue: \emph{stagnation}. During the iterative process, pheromone values can become overly concentrated on certain edges. This concentration collapses exploration, causing the colony to repeatedly reconstruct the same (often suboptimal) structures and become trapped in local minima. While a number of techniques mitigate stagnation (restarts, smoothing, pheromone reinitialization, diversification rules), they are typically \emph{expert-designed}, require careful tuning, and may not adapt well across varying instance geometries, scales, and feature distributions.

\paragraph{Why not static neural priors?}
Recent work has attempted to ``neuralize'' ACO by replacing or augmenting its heuristic matrix with a learned heatmap, or by predicting both heuristic and initial pheromone matrices. Such methods can improve performance on small instances, and inspired follow-ups focusing on more efficient training signals and sampling. A shared limitation, however, is that they operate largely in an \emph{open-loop} manner: the learned output is static (or fixed at initialization), and training often ignores core ACO feedback signals such as pheromone evolution and best-so-far improvements. As a result, these methods cannot \emph{react} to the dynamic stochastic process that defines ACO's power and its failure mode (stagnation).

\paragraph{Our proposal: closed-loop neural control of ACO.}
We introduce \emph{Closed-Loop ACO} (CL-ACO), which trains a neural policy to control the ACO trajectory online. The controller is conditioned on the current pheromone state and lightweight progress indicators, and it reshapes transition probabilities through a residual factor, providing a flexible mechanism to guide exploration/exploitation without hard-coded rules. We train using \emph{snapshot-based reinforcement learning}: from a saved solver state (snapshot), we roll out a short continuation horizon under baseline ACO and under neural-guided ACO from the same snapshot and paired randomness. The reward is the improvement of neural continuation relative to baseline continuation, which targets precisely the ability to escape local minima and improve trajectory quality.

\paragraph{Training distribution: frozen generations with mixed replay.}
A practical challenge in snapshot-based training is that the snapshot distribution is itself policy-dependent: as the controller improves, it visits different ACO states. Naively regenerating the entire dataset frequently can create a moving target and unstable training dynamics. We therefore adopt a DAgger-style approach: we collect snapshots in discrete \emph{generations} and keep each generation fixed on disk. Training uses a replay mixture of all generations collected so far (optionally balanced per generation), and new data is added only between training phases.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{Closed-loop controller for ACO.} A residual policy that conditions on pheromone statistics and search progress to steer the stochastic reconstruction process online.
  \item \textbf{Snapshot continuation training.} A training scheme that samples solver snapshots and optimizes continuation quality over a horizon $H$, directly targeting stagnation recovery.
  \item \textbf{Low-variance learning machinery.} Baseline-vs-Baseline reward, paired randomness via traced sampling, baseline caching, and per-ant credit assignment for stable policy gradients.
  \item \textbf{A practical ACO backbone.} We build on a modern ACO variant with candidate lists, bounded pheromones, and local search, and show how to integrate neural control with minimal disruption.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
% =========================================================

\paragraph{Neural combinatorial optimization.}
Constructive NCO methods learn a direct mapping from an instance to a complete solution, often using attention-based sequence decoders or graph neural networks. Hybrid/improvement methods instead learn actions that improve a given solution or guide a heuristic process, such as predicting local moves (e.g., k-opt) or scoring neighborhoods to accelerate search. Our work belongs to the hybrid family: we \emph{control} an existing metaheuristic trajectory rather than replacing the solver.

\paragraph{Ant Colony Optimization and stagnation.}
ACO constructs solutions via stochastic transitions biased by pheromone and heuristic signals. Large-scale effectiveness often relies on candidate lists, pheromone bounding (e.g., MMAS), and embedded local search. Stagnation is a known weakness, addressed by expert-designed mechanisms such as pheromone smoothing, restarts, or colony resets. These mechanisms are powerful but instance-sensitive and require tuning.

\paragraph{Neural-augmented ACO.}
Recent approaches learn a heuristic matrix (heatmap) or initialization signals intended to improve ACO. Follow-up work has proposed more efficient training rollouts or to jointly output multiple priors. Most of these approaches are effectively \emph{open-loop}: the learned signals are static during the run, and training signals may omit the dynamics of pheromone evolution and best-so-far feedback. In contrast, CL-ACO is \emph{closed-loop}, conditioning on the evolving solver state and acting throughout the process.

\section{Background: ACO Backbone}
% =========================================================

We describe the ACO backbone used in this work, which is representative of modern high-performance variants. We focus on Euclidean TSP, but the framework naturally extends to VRP.

\subsection{Problem definition}
Given $n$ nodes with coordinates $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n$, the TSP seeks a Hamiltonian cycle minimizing total length. Let $d(i,j)$ denote Euclidean distance. A tour $\pi$ is a permutation of nodes; the objective is
\begin{equation}
  C(\pi) = \sum_{t=1}^{n-1} d(\pi_t,\pi_{t+1}) + d(\pi_n,\pi_1).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Sparse candidate lists}
Instead of considering all $n-1$ next nodes, each node $u$ maintains a candidate list of size $k$ (nearest neighbors). We denote candidate index $j \in \{1,\dots,k\}$ mapping to neighbor $v_{u,j}$. A backup list (size $bl$) is used if candidates are exhausted.

\subsection{Transition weights and sampling}
ACO assigns each candidate edge $(u,v)$ a weight combining pheromone and heuristic:
\begin{equation}
  w(u,v) = \tau(u,v)^\alpha \cdot \eta(u,v) \cdot \exp(r(u,v)),
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is pheromone, $\eta$ is heuristic (often $1/d$), and $r$ is an optional residual term. Classical ACO uses $r\equiv 0$. When heuristic is disabled, we set $\eta \equiv 1$, yielding pheromone-driven search.

At each stochastic decision, an ant chooses among valid unvisited candidates by roulette-wheel sampling proportional to $w(u,v)$ (falling back to uniform sampling in numerical underflow cases).

\subsection{Update rules and pheromone bounds}
After sampling a batch of $A$ ants, the algorithm updates pheromones using the best sampled tour (or global best). We use MMAS-style bounds:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item Evaporation: $\tau \leftarrow (1-\rho)\tau$ and clamp to $[\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max}]$.
  \item Deposit on best tour edges: $\tau \leftarrow \min(\tau+\Delta, \tau_{\max})$, with $\Delta \propto 1/C$.
  \item Trail limits $(\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max})$ depend on the current best cost.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Local search integration}
To improve solution quality, we embed a restricted 2-opt local search that focuses on a small checklist of nodes affected by ``new edges'' introduced during sampling. This preserves speed while capturing most local improvements.

\subsection{Stagnation intuition}
Stagnation arises when pheromone concentrates: for many nodes, one candidate dominates $w(u,v)$, and the decision entropy collapses. This reduces the chance of exploring alternative edges that could unlock better tours, causing the colony to circulate near a local optimum.

% =========================================================
\section{Closed-Loop Ant Colony Optimization (CL-ACO)}
% =========================================================

Our key idea is to treat ACO as a controllable stochastic process and learn a policy that reshapes transition probabilities online.

\subsection{From open-loop priors to closed-loop control}
Open-loop neural ACO methods typically output a static heuristic matrix (heatmap) or initial pheromone matrix. Such priors can help align ACO toward good structures \emph{on average}, but they cannot react when the colony becomes overconfident in a suboptimal structure.

CL-ACO instead introduces a \emph{controller} that is active throughout the run and conditions on the current solver state (especially pheromone). This enables adaptive behavior such as temporarily encouraging exploration when pheromone becomes too concentrated, or sharpening exploitation when multiple promising structures emerge.

\subsection{Residual policy parameterization}
We incorporate the controller by adding a residual logit to each candidate edge weight (Eq.~\ref{eq:weight}). For node $u$ and its candidate neighbor $v_{u,j}$:
\begin{equation}
  r_\theta(u, v_{u,j}) = \gamma \cdot f_\theta\Big(h_u, h_{v_{u,j}}, \; d(u,v_{u,j}), \; \psi(\tau_{u,:}), \; c \Big),
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item $h_i$ are node embeddings from a GNN encoder over the $k$NN graph;
  \item $d(u,v)$ is the Euclidean distance feature for the candidate edge;
  \item $\psi(\tau_{u,:})$ summarizes the \emph{current pheromone row} for node $u$ (dynamic features);
  \item $c$ is lightweight context such as stagnation level and progress;
  \item $\gamma$ is a residual scale controlling how strongly the policy perturbs ACO.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Dynamic pheromone features.}
In our implementation, $\psi(\tau_{u,:})$ is formed from inexpensive row-wise statistics computed on the sparse pheromone matrix:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item $\log\tau$ z-score within row: $(\log\tau - \mu)/\sigma$;
  \item rank of $\tau$ within row, scaled to $[0,1]$;
  \item broadcast scalar features: normalized stagnation and progress.
\end{itemize}
These features provide a compact signal describing pheromone concentration and relative edge prominence.

\paragraph{Why a residual?}
The residual multiplies ACO's native weights by $\exp(r)$, preserving ACO's structure and stability. The controller does not need to rebuild tours from scratch; it only biases ACO's existing sampling mechanism.

\subsection{Closed-loop execution}
During each ACO iteration, pheromones change, which changes $\psi(\tau)$ and thus $r_\theta$. The policy therefore adapts to the current state of the search rather than relying on a fixed prior.

\section{Learning: Snapshot-Based Reinforcement Learning}
% =========================================================

We train $f_\theta$ to improve continuation quality from intermediate ACO states. The central target is: \emph{given a snapshot of the ACO process, control the next $H$ iterations to improve the best-so-far cost compared to baseline ACO.}

\subsection{Snapshot dataset}
A snapshot stores everything needed to resume ACO from an intermediate time step:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item instance data: coordinates, distances;
  \item solver state: sparse pheromone $\tau$, current source tour/cost, best tour/cost, candidate lists;
  \item metadata: iteration index, no-improve counter, pheromone bounds.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Iterative snapshot generations.}
Rather than maintaining a single static dataset or regenerating data every epoch, we use a DAgger-style iterative data pipeline with \emph{frozen generations}. Generation 0 (Gen-0) is created by running baseline ACO on many random instances and saving solver state periodically after a warmup. After training on the current mixture of generations, we create Gen-$g{>}0$ by running \emph{model-guided} ACO under the current policy on the same instance distribution and saving snapshots. Each generation is stored on disk and remains fixed; training samples from a mixture of Gen-0..Gen-$g$.

This ``frozen generations + replay'' design keeps the dataset distribution stable within each training phase while still exposing the controller to states induced by its own behavior over time.

\paragraph{Warmup schedule.}
To avoid collecting only very early, uninformative states, snapshots are saved only after a warmup threshold. In our implementation, warmup can optionally increase with generation: $\text{warmup}(g)=\text{warmup}_0 + g\,\Delta$, capped to $[0,T-1]$ where $T$ is the snapshot-generation iteration budget.

\subsection{Continuation objective and Baseline-vs-Baseline reward}
For a snapshot $s$, define baseline continuation cost $C_{\text{base}}(s;H)$ as the best cost after running baseline ACO for $H$ iterations from $s$, and neural continuation cost $C_\theta(s;H)$ similarly. We define a normalized continuation reward:
\begin{equation}
  R(s) = \frac{C_{\text{base}}(s;H)-C_\theta(s;H)}{C_{\text{base}}(s;H)+\eps}.
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
This reward directly measures improvement relative to the solver from the same starting state.

\subsection{Paired randomness via traced sampling}
ACO is highly stochastic. To reduce variance, we use \emph{paired randomness}: baseline and neural continuations are run with the same RNG seed and (best-effort) identical RNG consumption. We implement this by using a traced sampling mode that records the decision trace and supports deterministic replay of log-probabilities under the neural-adjusted distribution.

\subsection{Per-ant credit assignment}
A continuation comprises many decisions across many ants. We record per-ant log-probabilities and compute per-ant rewards by pairing each ant's best tour cost in the neural rollout with the corresponding ant's best tour cost in the baseline rollout. This yields a reward matrix $R_{b,a}$ for snapshot $b$ and ant $a$, improving credit assignment and stability.

\subsection{Policy gradient (REINFORCE)}
Let $\log \pi_\theta$ denote the (replayed) sum of log-probabilities for the stochastic decisions. We normalize by the number of stochastic decisions to stabilize gradients across different horizons. The REINFORCE loss is:
\begin{equation}
  \calL_{\pi}(\theta) = - \E\big[ A \cdot \log \pi_\theta \big],
\end{equation}
where $A$ is an advantage (e.g., reward minus batch mean).
We use an entropy proxy computed from the current sparse probability matrix as a regularizer (noting it is an approximation due to visited masks).

\section{Algorithms}
% =========================================================

This section mirrors the core implementation. We provide two algorithms: neural continuation from a snapshot and the overall training loop.

\subsection{Neural residual continuation from a snapshot}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Neural Residual FACO Continuation (CL-ACO)}
\label{alg:continuation}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Snapshot $s$, horizon $H$, policy network $f_\theta$, residual scale $\gamma$, seed $\xi$
\Ensure Best cost after continuation $C_\theta$, and log-probabilities for policy gradient
\State Restore ACO solver state from snapshot $s$
\State Set RNG seed to $\xi$ (paired randomness)
\State Build $k$NN graph and compute node embeddings $\{h_i\}$ once (cache)
\State Synchronize pheromone $\tau$ to accelerator (if applicable)
\State Initialize totals: $\log p \leftarrow 0$, entropy $\leftarrow 0$, ant-best costs $\leftarrow +\infty$
\For{$t=1$ to $H$}
  \State Compute dynamic pheromone features $\psi(\tau)$ (row-wise $\log\tau$ z-score, rank, context)
  \State For each candidate edge $(u,v_{u,j})$, compute residual logits $r_\theta(u,v_{u,j})$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:residual}
  \State Sample $A$ ants using weights $w(u,v)=\tau(u,v)^\alpha \eta(u,v)\exp(r_\theta(u,v))$, recording trace
  \State Replay trace to compute differentiable per-ant log-probabilities under $\pi_\theta$
  \State Update per-ant best costs and update global pheromone using iteration-best tour
\EndFor
\State \Return $C_\theta$ (best cost), per-ant log-prob sums, per-ant costs, entropy proxy
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Snapshot-based training with BvB reward and baseline cache}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Snapshot RL Training with Baseline-vs-Baseline Reward and Frozen Generations}
\label{alg:train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Gen-0 snapshot dataset $\calD_0$, fixed horizon $H$, policy net $f_\theta$, baseline cache, optimizer
\For{generation $g = 0,1,\dots$}
  \If{$g>0$}
    \State Collect new snapshots $\calD_g$ by running model-guided ACO under the current $f_\theta$ (frozen on disk)
  \EndIf
  \State Form replay mixture $\calD \leftarrow \cup_{i=0}^{g} \calD_i$ (optionally balanced per generation)
  \For{epoch $=1,2,\dots,E$ (optionally early-stop on validation)}
    \State Sample a mini-batch of snapshots $\{s\}$ from $\calD$
    \For{each snapshot $s$ in batch}
      \State Choose base seed $\xi \leftarrow \textsc{StableHash}(s, H)$
      \State Query cache for baseline continuation costs from $(s,H,\xi)$
      \If{cache miss}
        \State Run baseline continuation for $H$ steps in traced mode, store best cost and per-ant best costs
      \EndIf
      \State Run neural continuation from $s$ for $H$ steps with the same seed $\xi$ (Alg.~\ref{alg:continuation})
      \State Compute per-ant rewards via Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward} (paired baseline vs neural)
    \EndFor
    \State Compute REINFORCE loss with entropy regularization
    \State Backpropagate, clip gradients, optimizer step
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Implementation Details}
% =========================================================

We summarize key engineering choices reflected in the provided implementation.

\subsection{ACO backbone: MFACO with candidate lists and local search}
The solver maintains:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item sparse pheromone matrix $\tau \in \R^{n \times k}$ aligned with $k$NN edges;
  \item candidate list $\texttt{nn\_list}[u,j]=v$ and inverse positions $\texttt{nn\_pos}$ for fast updates;
  \item source and global-best tours and their costs;
  \item MMAS-style bounded pheromone update with $\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max}$ depending on best cost.
\end{itemize}
Sampling constructs tours by applying a relocation-style operator for a limited number of ``new edges'' followed by restricted 2-opt, yielding high-quality local improvements at low cost.

\subsection{Graph construction and neural scorer}
We represent the instance as a directed $k$NN graph $\calG$ consistent with the solver candidate edges. The network consists of:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item a GNN encoder that produces node embeddings $h_i$;
  \item an edge scorer MLP that takes $(h_u, h_v, d(u,v), \text{extra feats})$ and outputs a scalar logit.
\end{itemize}
Node embeddings are computed once per snapshot. Dynamic features derived from pheromone are recomputed each iteration.

\subsection{Traced sampling and differentiable replay}
To obtain gradients for decisions made inside a C++ sampler, we:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item sample tours in traced mode (recording chosen nodes and masks);
  \item reconstruct differentiable log-probabilities by replaying the trace under the current probability matrix computed in torch.
\end{itemize}
This decouples fast sampling from differentiable policy gradient computation.

\subsection{Baseline caching}
Baseline continuation from a snapshot can be expensive. We cache baseline results keyed by $(\texttt{snapshot\_id}, H, \texttt{seed})$, storing both best cost and per-ant best costs. This reduces training variance and cost, especially when a snapshot is sampled multiple times.

\subsection{Frozen replay generations (DAgger-style)}
To keep the training distribution stable while still adapting data to the current policy, we store snapshots as discrete generations on disk. Gen-0 is baseline ACO; Gen-$g{>}0$ is generated by model-guided ACO using the policy trained on earlier generations. Training samples from a mixture of generations (optionally balanced per generation) and new generations are added only after the model has converged on the current mixture.

In our implementation, each generation is trained for up to a fixed number of epochs $E$, and convergence-gated dataset expansion is optionally enabled via a patience criterion on a scalar performance metric (e.g., validation improvement).

\section{Experimental Setup}
% =========================================================

We evaluate CL-ACO on Euclidean TSP and report both continuation improvements and full-run improvements.

\subsection{Datasets}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{Training:} random Euclidean TSP instances generated on-the-fly; snapshots are stored as frozen generations. Gen-0 is collected from baseline ACO; later generations are collected from model-guided ACO under the current policy. Training uses a replay mixture of Gen-0..Gen-$g$.
  \item \textbf{Validation/Test:} fixed coordinate tensors (e.g., \texttt{valDataset-$n$.pt}, \texttt{testDataset-$n$.pt}) to ensure consistent comparisons across runs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baselines}
We compare:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{Vanilla FACO/MFACO:} same hyperparameters and compute budget, no residual ($r \equiv 0$).
  \item \textbf{Ablations of CL-ACO:} removing dynamic features, removing paired randomness, disabling local search, static residual, etc. (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}).
  \item \textbf{Static-prior variants (optional):} a learned static heatmap injected as heuristic (open-loop), to match prior neural ACO paradigms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation protocols}
\paragraph{Continuation evaluation.}
Given a snapshot $s$ and horizon $H$, compare $C_\theta(s;H)$ vs $C_{\text{base}}(s;H)$ using paired seeds. This directly tests the ability to escape stagnation from intermediate states.

\paragraph{Full-run evaluation.}
Initialize solver from scratch and run for a fixed number of iterations. Compare final best costs baseline vs neural-guided ACO. This measures end-to-end impact.

\subsection{Metrics}
We report:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item mean best cost and mean \% improvement over baseline;
  \item improvement as a function of stagnation level (snapshot bins);
  \item variance across seeds and runtime overhead.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
% =========================================================

\paragraph{Why closed-loop matters.}
The defining failure mode of ACO (stagnation) is intrinsically dynamic: it arises from the feedback loop between sampling and pheromone updates. Open-loop priors cannot respond when the run deviates from the ``average'' trajectory. CL-ACO addresses this by conditioning on pheromone concentration and search progress, enabling adaptive exploration/exploitation shifts.

\paragraph{Compute and training cost.}
Snapshot continuation training can be expensive due to baseline rollouts. Baseline caching and short horizons amortize this cost. Paired randomness reduces gradient variance, reducing the number of samples required for stable learning.

\paragraph{Entropy proxy approximation.}
Our entropy regularization uses an approximate entropy computed from the full sparse probability matrix rather than the exact masked distribution per step. This is sufficient as a weak regularizer but could be improved by step-level entropy tracking.

\paragraph{Generalization to VRP.}
Extending CL-ACO to VRP is natural: the same control principle applies, with state features incorporating capacity, depot structure, and route segmentation. The snapshot RL machinery remains unchanged.

\paragraph{Future directions.}
Promising directions include learning additional control actions (e.g., pheromone smoothing parameters, restart triggers), richer state encodings (explicit stagnation detectors), and multi-objective control balancing solution quality and runtime.

\section{Conclusion}
% =========================================================

We presented CL-ACO, a closed-loop neural controller for Ant Colony Optimization trained via snapshot-based reinforcement learning. Unlike prior neural ACO methods that learn static priors, CL-ACO conditions on the evolving pheromone state and search progress to reshape transition probabilities online, directly addressing stagnation without expert-designed restart rules. Our training approach uses Baseline-vs-Baseline continuation rewards, paired randomness through traced sampling, baseline caching, and per-ant credit assignment to achieve stable learning.
